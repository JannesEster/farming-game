# Camps, water and handling infrastructure on a Namibian extensive cattle farm

Teaching note for the Farming Game bot. Researched 12 Nov 2026 (Perth time) by Teacher Bot.

Scope: how a commercial, fenced, freehold cattle farm of about 3,300 to 13,000 ha in Namibia lays out its waterpoints, camps, lick, kraals, crush, loading ramp, gates, lanes and roads, and what the workers do every day and week. South African and Australian figures appear only as comparisons and are labelled as such.

How to read this note:

* **UNVERIFIED** means I could not confirm it from a primary source, or I only saw a search summary.
* **Common practice, not sourced** means it is widely described but I found no document that states it. Treat it as a design assumption.
* Prices from the NAU manuals are 2008 to 2011 figures. They are dated and only useful as ratios.

## 0. The farm in one picture (a real example)

The best documented Namibian layout I found is Farm Springbockvley, 180 km south east of Windhoek, on the western edge of the Kalahari, 9,500 ha, about 260 mm average rain [4]. It is a holistic (planned grazing) farm, so it has more camps than an average farm, but it shows every element clearly:

* **Camps:** 60 more or less rectangular camps, average 160 ha, smallest 45 ha, largest 330 ha [4].
* **Cells:** three big cells of 16 to 17 camps each (up to 2,000 sheep plus 300 cattle per cell) and a fourth cell of 11 camps around the farmyard for a smaller mixed herd (about 500 sheep and 100 cattle) [4].
* **Water:** 17 water points, each a reservoir plus troughs, and **each water point is surrounded by 4 to 5 camps** [4].
* **Boreholes:** 7 boreholes, 5 to 25 m deep, all on windmills, no engines. Water gravitates through about 25 km of buried 40 mm plastic pipe from the reservoirs at the boreholes to the other water points [4].
* **Troughs:** two troughs of 3 m each, gravity fed from the reservoir through short 50 mm pipes, with the ball valve outside the kraal so cattle cannot break it, are enough for 2,000 sheep and 300 cattle to drink comfortably [4].
* **Handling:** two handling facilities on the farm and a loading ramp near the farmstead, which is 1.5 km from the public road [4].
* **People:** 4 permanent staff, each responsible for one of the four mixed herds. They reach the animals and water points on foot, by car, tractor or motorbike, and the owner uses horses [4].
* **Routine:** "all flerds are visited and checked for wellbeing, efficient water and lick supply at least every second day", and a lot of time goes into checking border fences [4].

A more ordinary benchmark: the NAU/NNFU mechanics manual describes "a normal 5,000 ha farm with normal shape" as having about 30 km of border fence plus 50 km of inner fences, and three fully equipped water installations feeding several camps through pipelines, reservoirs and troughs [3]. The national rangeland policy's economic case study used a typical commercial base case of 10,000 ha fenced into **four camps** with about 750 cattle, in poor veld condition [5]. So real farms range from a handful of big camps to 60 small ones.

## 1. Waterpoints

### 1.1 Where the water comes from

* **Boreholes are the backbone.** Namibia had about 51,500 recorded boreholes, 95% shallower than 200 m (2006 study for the Ministry of Mines and Energy) [9]. The ministry summary says more than 40,000 are in use for households, farming and tourism [8].
* Depth varies hugely. Springbockvley's are 5 to 25 m [4]. Irrigation boreholes at Stampriet (Bonsmara farm, 40,000 ha) are typically 100 to 110 m and must be cased and cemented to keep saline water out; abstraction is metered and water levels are reported monthly [35].
* **Earth dams** are used where runoff is high, for example the Khomas Hochland mountains, where farmers rely on them for stock water [3].
* **Excavation dams (gatdamme)** are common on the flats: a big hole dug where water flows in the rains. Depending on size and soil they can hold stock water for up to a year [3].
* **Sand storage dams** (a concrete wall in a rocky gorge, raised about 50 cm at a time as sand fills behind it) lose almost nothing to evaporation. The manual says open earth dams are only 11 to 15% effective by comparison [3].
* **Livestock should not drink straight from an earth dam.** Fence the dam off and pipe the water to a trough, because of heavy parasite loads and dung build up [2][3].

### 1.2 Pumps: windmill vs solar vs diesel vs grid

| Driver | Sourced strengths | Sourced weaknesses | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Windmill (wind pump) | Historically the main pump on off grid Namibian farms. Long service life, lifts from 300 to 400 m, no fuel, basic skills, good local service network. Reputation for being reliable with fairly low maintenance if head size is matched to depth and demand | Not simple to install. Needs more storage than diesel or solar to ride out calm periods. Work intensive to maintain | [3][9] |
| Solar submersible | No fuel, low maintenance (pump service every 3 to 5 years per the 2006 study). Pumps 8 to 10 sunshine hours a day, so it suits weak boreholes. Ministry summary: about 10,000 L/day from 100 m, about 20,000 L/day from 50 m. On low yield boreholes (3 to 5 m³/day) it was cheaper than diesel from day one. No batteries needed. Panels face north at about 25 degrees in the north of Namibia and 35 degrees in the south; tracking adds 40% or more | Higher upfront cost. Panel theft is a serious problem and the manual says insurance for it was no longer available. Only pumps when the sun shines | [3][8] |
| Diesel or petrol engine with power head | Large power range, water on demand. Used where there is little wind (deep ravines) or very deep boreholes | Fuel cost, high maintenance and replacement | [3][8] |
| Grid (NamPower) or generator | Easy submersible install where grid exists. A mobile genset can serve several pumps in turn | Generators waste energy converting mechanical to electrical and back | [3] |

* Solar vs diesel break even table (Scholle 2006, in [3]): at shallow depth and low volume solar wins at once; break even stretches to several years at higher volumes, and at the highest volumes and depths diesel stays cheaper. Use it as a shape, not a number, because fuel and panel prices have changed since 2006.
* **Pump sizing rule:** test the borehole and size the pump to normally no more than 60% of its long term delivery [3].
* Theft: a news report says the Agriculture Ministry lost N$120 million to water pump and solar panel theft (search summary only, **UNVERIFIED**, I did not read the article) [29].
* Trend: Common practice, not sourced: many farms now run a mix of old windmills and newer solar pumps. I found no national survey of how many farms use each.

### 1.3 Reservoirs, troughs and pipelines

* **Drinking need:** adult cattle drink about 50 L a day [2]; the mechanics manual says "up to 50 litres", and 5 L per head for small stock [3]. Heat raises intake; I found no Namibian figure for hot day peaks (gap).
* **Reservoir size:** hold enough water for **at least 2 weeks** for the herds it serves. Smaller tanks can work, but then any breakdown (windmill, pipeline, engine) must be fixed immediately [3]. NAU Large Stock says reservoirs should cover "a few days" [2]. Windmills need extra storage for calm spells [3].
* **Keep empty reservoirs at least 30 cm deep** when camps are not in use, so the concrete does not crack [3].
* Do not let reservoirs leak or overflow for long: foundations sag, animals dig under them and cattle fight and damage the structure. Remove nearby trees (roots lift the floor) [3].
* Wildlife safety: an old fence pole leaning into the water lets baboons climb out; a floating tyre stops birds drowning, which matters for salmonella and EU export beef [3].
* **Troughs:**
  * Every camp must have free access to water, either a trough in each camp or a trough in a central water kraal shared by camps used in rotation by one herd [2].
  * If water flows in fast, a big trough is not needed [2][3]. At Springbockvley recharge speed matters more than stored volume [4].
  * Round troughs with a ball valve in a centre tower hold a lot and keep water fresh all round, which avoids crowding at the valve end of long troughs, but they are hard to clean [3].
  * Calves and sheep must not be able to fall in and drown [2][3].
  * Pave or harden a footing of at least one cattle length (about 2 m) around the trough to stop trampling out, especially on sand [3].
  * Raised troughs collect less dust [2][3].
* **Pipelines:** the choice between drilling another borehole and laying a pipeline depends on drilling success odds and cost [3]. Blue PVC must be buried at least 30 cm (not UV resistant); black polyethylene is more UV resistant but is better buried or covered with stones; pipe hung on a fence should be painted with aluminium paint [3]. Water from an above ground pipe should go into a reservoir before the trough so it can cool, because it can be "steaming hot" in summer [3]. Air valves go on the high points; mole rats and porcupines chew buried pipe at small leaks [3].
* **Water law:** under the Water Resources Management Act (11 of 2013) the NAU advises that all boreholes must be registered with MAWLR, including dry ones and windmill boreholes for livestock, and that an abstraction and use licence is needed for all boreholes except domestic use. "Domestic use" includes watering animals for reasonable own use [21][22]. Whether a commercial cattle herd counts as "reasonable own use" is not settled in what I read (**UNVERIFIED**).

### 1.4 How many camps share one waterpoint

* The NAU ideal is water in the middle of each camp, but it says this is usually not financially viable [1].
* The sourced solution is to share water: a trough in a central water kraal for adjacent camps grazed in rotation by one herd [2]. Springbockvley has 4 to 5 camps around each water point [4].
* South African comparison: on multi camp systems water is often supplied at one central point; ProAgri reports that a watering camp with one trough for the whole system led to **less trampling** than giving each camp around the centre its own trough, with good management [13].
* Communal comparison (NAU): short barrier fences (wire or bush) running 100 to 300 m out from a shared water point funnel each herd into its own sector and stop milling around the water [1].
* Consequence: shared water creates a "wagon wheel" layout. Camps are wedges or rectangles that meet at the water kraal. NAU warns against long narrow camps, especially when water cannot be central, because the near end is overused and the far corners underused [1]. Place water in the less preferred part of a camp to pull animals there [1].

### 1.5 Walking distance and the piosphere

"Piosphere" is the zone of impact around a water point: bare and trampled near the water, fading with distance.

Namibian evidence:
* Northern Kalahari communal water points (Otjinene, Katjiua and Ward 2012): nothing grew within about 30 m of the water; Zone I (sparse, browse line) ran to about 850 m; Zone II 850 to 2,000 m; Zone III (bush proliferation) to about 3,000 m; Zone IV 3,000 to 4,000 m transitional; Zone V 4,000 to 5,000 m more open [6]. Homesteads and pens sat 300 to 600 m from the water [6].
* In the same area, cattle "can easily graze beyond 7,000 m from water points" but must return to water every other day [7].
* The thesis notes that the then Ministry of Agriculture, Water and Forestry spaced communal water points 7,000 m apart, and argues more than 10,000 m would reduce degradation and keep grazing reserves between settlements [7].
* National policy: in most sites in the Okondjatu area no perennial grasses were found within 2 km of the water point [5]. Animals walking in long lines to and from water trample paths to dust [5].
* Modelling for the southern Kalahari (Jeltsch et al. 1997): piosphere zones of bare soil, shrubs and mixed savanna expand outward at a rate tied to grazing pressure, and shrub encroached zones barely recover within 100 years after cattle are removed [20].

Distance guidelines (none is an official Namibian standard):

| Guideline | Figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| South Africa (ProAgri, extension style) | One water point per camp is enough if animals walk no more than 2,000 m; preferably not more than 1,000 m. More walking costs energy and feed | [12] |
| USA arid rangeland (Holechek, in Bailey) | Count land 1.6 to 3.2 km from water at half value for stocking; land beyond 3.2 km as ungrazable | [19] |
| Northern Australia (MLA) | Cattle will walk up to 10 km from water, but up to 80% of grazing is within 2 km | [15] |
| Northern Australia (Hunt, MLA) | Waters about 5 km apart and not more than 6 km (grazing radius 2.5 to 3 km); about one water per 20 to 25 km²; ideally no more than 300 head per water point | [16][34] |
| Western Australia southern rangelands | Grazing range rarely exceeds 5 km, so waters no more than 10 km apart, preferably away from fence lines | [17] |
| South Australian workbook | About 5 km is where walking costs more energy than the grazing returns; aim for most grazing within 3 km | [18] |
| Intensive humid pasture (Kentucky, Canada), only for contrast | 240 m (800 ft) | [33] |

**Take away:** on a fenced Namibian camp farm, walking distances are short because camps are small (a 160 ha square camp is about 1.26 km across, so nowhere is more than about 1.8 km from a corner trough; my arithmetic). The piosphere problem shows up as overused trough corners and lanes. The 2 to 7 km distances are for big open camps and communal land.

## 2. Lick

### 2.1 What lick, when

* **Summer (green season): phosphate lick.** NAU: phosphorus is short in all parts of Namibia for most of the year; on green veld use a summer lick, sold as P6 (6% phosphorus) ready mix or P12 premix to be mixed with salt [2]. Phosphates taste bad, so salt and some energy are added to get intake [2]. A Namibian supplier adds that on green veld phosphorus is the first limiting nutrient and that urea free phosphate licks are safe in the rain [11].
* **Winter (dry season): protein or production lick.** Protein is limiting and intake is low; both are addressed with urea (or other non protein nitrogen) licks, with some natural protein and fast energy, some sulphur, and some phosphorus for lactating and late pregnant cows [2]. NAU examples: eco-maintenance lick, maxi-maintenance lick, dry veld concentrate plus salt, cattle mix plus urea plus salt (plus pods) [2].
* **Pods:** camel thorn and prosopis pods are often milled into licks. Camel thorn pods: 8% digestible protein; prosopis pods: 17%. Camel thorn pods contain prussic acid, especially green [2].
* **Bush areas:** licks with ingredients that help digest browse exist [2].
* **Changeover dates (NAU calendar example):** week 1 of January "decide on changeover to summer lick and fast rotational grazing"; week 1 of April "decide on changeover to winter lick and slower rotational grazing" [2]. NAU adds that the switch depends on when rains start and stop [2].
* Real farm example: Springbockvley feeds a minimal self mixed lick, phosphate, salt and sulphur in summer and salt, urea, molasses and maize bran in the non growing season; its sheep get no lick because the trough design keeps them out [4].

### 2.2 Intake figures (one supplier's label guidance, not a standard)

From the Feedmaster 2025 product guide [10] (Feedmaster is a Namibian supplier; always use the label of the product actually used):

| Lick | Season | Cattle intake per head per day | Small stock |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Futterfos P6 (phosphate, ready mix) | green grazing | 100 to 200 g | 17 to 50 g |
| Cattle Lick 40 (ready mix, contains NPN) | dry grazing | 375 g | not for small stock |
| Eco-Grasveld lick (self mix) | dry grazing | 500 g | 85 g |
| Cow calf lick (self mix) | dry grazing | 600 g | 100 g |
| Energy lick | when protein licks are not enough, or production | 1,000 to 1,500 g | 250 to 400 g |

* Bags in that guide are 50 kg [10].
* So a herd of 300 cattle on a 375 g/day winter lick eats about 112 kg a day, about two 50 kg bags plus (my arithmetic, for the sim only).

### 2.3 How often to fill, safety

* NAU: a urea lick should be available **every day**, because rumen microbes adapt to it; "feeding any lick on an irregular basis is a waste of money" [2]. Start urea licks slowly [2].
* Feedmaster: keep troughs filled to avoid gluttony and urea poisoning; feed a salt phosphate lick for at least 7 days before any NPN lick [10].
* Urea dissolves in rain water collected in a trough; animals can drink it and die, so protect lick from rain or drill drain holes [2][30].
* Intake control: more salt reduces intake; molasses or a little salt raises it; grit or block form also slows intake [2].
* Fill frequency in practice: Springbockvley checks lick (and water) at least every second day [4]. Common practice, not sourced: lick runs once or twice a week with the bakkie, topping up troughs to cover the days in between. I found no Namibian source with a standard fill interval.

### 2.4 Where lick troughs go

* **Trough type (NAU):** half drums cut lengthwise with a footing so they do not roll, drums cut across into two pots, or tyres turned inside out with a metal, cement or rubber base, which last almost forever. Protect lick from rain or drill holes in the bottom [2].
* **Trough space:** NAU says space at a lick trough "seems to be irrelevant", unlike feed troughs, which need about 40 cm per animal [2].
* **Placement for grazing distribution:**
  * KZN Department of Agriculture: use watering points and supplements to make animals move and graze different areas, especially in big camps [14].
  * US arid rangeland research (Bailey): strategic supplement placement far from water raised forage use by 14% up to 600 m from the supplement in foothill rangeland [19].
* **Where Namibian farmers actually put them:** Common practice, not sourced: lick troughs are usually at or near the water point or water kraal, sometimes under a small roof, because that is where the herd comes daily and the bakkie can reach them. I found no Namibian guide that states a placement rule. Moving lick away from water is a sourced distribution tool [14][19], but not a sourced Namibian norm.

## 3. Kraals, crush pens and loading ramps

### 3.1 What the facility contains (NAU design rules [2])

* Plan for current and future herd size, the facilities needed (loading ramp, dip, AI facilities, cattle scale, neck clamp, calf crush), how many groups are handled at once, horns and temperament.
* **Gates always in a corner of a pen**, hinged to lie against the fence when open. 2.4 m gates for cattle, 3 m where vehicles pass. Close with chains, because cattle bend gates and fixed latches stop fitting.
* The funnel into the crush has one straight side; two angled sides cause milling.
* Build of wood or steel (higher cost, lower maintenance). Poles 3.5 m apart, 2.5 m at pressure points, 1.5 to 1.8 m high. **Floor space 3.2 m² per animal** in handling pens.
* **Crush:** 6 to 18 m long (not longer), ideally facing south so cattle are never driven into the sun, uprights not more than 1.8 m apart, internal width 70 to 75 cm. Calf crush 40 cm wide. A V shaped crush stops calves turning but cattle can climb on each other.
* Neck clamp at the front; body clamps optional. A cattle scale sits in or in front of the crush.
* **Loading ramp:** an extension of the crush, not steeper than 1:4, same internal width as the crush, with a level stretch before the climb; level loading is ideal if the terrain allows.

US extension comparison: site the corral with drainage, prevailing winds, nearby all weather roads and utilities in mind; if possible put the loading chute outside the corral and pasture so trucks stay out of the lots and spread less disease [27].

### 3.2 Where it sits

* Springbockvley: two handling facilities on the farm and a loading ramp near the farmstead, 1.5 km from the public road [4].
* NAU: calving can happen in small camps near the homestead for closer control; regular human and dog movement keeps predators off [2].
* Night kraaling near people is a predator measure (earlier lesson, farm-life section 3.6).
* Common practice, not sourced: the main kraal, crush and loading ramp sit near the homestead and on the farm's main access road, so a livestock truck can reach the ramp on a decent gravel road without crossing camps. Bigger farms add an outlying kraal or crush at a far water point. I found no Namibian siting standard.

### 3.3 Gathering and loading for sale

* **Gathering:** cattle come to water daily, so a water kraal with a gate is the natural catching point. Common practice, not sourced: workers close the water kraal gate when the herd is in to drink, then walk the herd down the lane or corridor to the main kraal. NAU corridors are made for moving cattle "with little labour" [2].
* **Low stress handling:** Springbockvley keeps crush work to a minimum (all cattle once a year for compulsory vaccination, plus branding, tagging and weighing before leaving the farm) and trains staff in low stress stockmanship: working from the side, using release of pressure, no sticks or shouting [4].
* **Paperwork before loading (brief, see the livestock skill for details):**
  * Animals must be registered on NamLITS; no livestock may move from or to an establishment that is not registered [23].
  * Every movement needs an Animal Movement Permit and Movement Notice from the State Veterinary office or NamLITS online, from the establishment of origin to the destination. If one truck loads from several farms, each farm needs its own permit. Return permit and notice to the State Vet within 21 days [23].
  * For Meatco slaughter, the producer is responsible for all documents on the truck; the 90/40 day verification report must accompany it; sick animals, injection marks and untagged animals are not accepted [24].
  * In the north (NCA), Meatco says only listed or pre selected cattle may be loaded after the permit, the truck or trailer must be cleaned, washed and disinfected at the farmer's cost, and welfare from loading to offloading is the farmer's and transporter's responsibility; payment comes about 10 days after purchase or slaughter [25].
  * Check the current FMD rules first: south of the VCF, auctions were suspended after the Sep 2026 Karasburg outbreak [32] (from the livestock skill).
* **Where cattle go:**
  * Auctions are a popular channel held countrywide on a regular basis [2]. Agra runs weaner auctions at auction pens around the country (Agra page blocked my fetch; detail from a search summary, **UNVERIFIED**) [26].
  * Direct to an abattoir such as Meatco; a farmer can use an agent or arrange transport and represent himself [2][24].
* Truck capacity per load and transport cost per km: **gap**, no sourced figure.

## 4. Gates, lanes, roads and moving herds

### 4.1 Fences

* Cattle camp fence: 1.2 m high with five steel wires; away from water and corners four wires will do [2][3].
* Posts normally 20 m apart with 5 droppers between; stronger fences (16 m, 4 droppers) are advisable near water points [3]. Straining posts not more than 500 m apart, fence straight between them [3].
* Jackal proof fencing is common on borders in sheep areas [3][4]. In a proclaimed jackal proof area a farmer can be forced to build it on the border [3].
* Game fences: 1.8 m for hartebeest, wildebeest and zebra; 2.4 m for kudu and eland. If wildebeest are kept, an extra fence 30 m inside the border is prescribed to stop malignant catarrhal fever spreading to neighbours' stock [3].
* Border fences are usually shared 50/50 by length between neighbours [3].
* Electric fences are "not commonly used in Namibia" for stock control because dry soil and long wire runs make single wire earth return systems inefficient; multi wire systems with alternate earth wires can work [3].
* Dated cost: about N$6,000 per km for a stock proof fence (manual published 2008, second edition 2011) [3].

### 4.2 Gates

* Gates always in the corner of a camp, not mid fence, so animals flow through easily; hinge them to lie against the fence when open [3].
* 2.5 m is enough for stock; 3 to 4.2 m for vehicles [3]. (NAU Large Stock gives 2.4 m and 3 m for pens [2].)
* At Springbockvley gates between empty camps are left open so game (oryx, springbok) can move [4].

### 4.3 Corridors (lanes)

* NAU: corridors move cattle with little labour, can serve as utility camps (sick animals) and, grazed clean, as firebreaks. About **15 m wide**, cleared of bush but with a few shade trees [2].
* Lanes concentrate dung and trampling. US extension (contrast only): with cattle walking down a lane to central water, about 15% of manure ends up in the lane [from the Missouri guide summary, **UNVERIFIED** figure, I did not open the PDF].

### 4.4 Roads and firebreaks

* Common practice, not sourced: two track gravel or sand farm roads (pads) follow fence lines so the bakkie can reach every gate, trough and lick point, and graded firebreaks often double as roads.
* NRMP: a drought reserve "spare camp" of at least 10% of the farm, rested each year, raises fire hazard, so firebreaks and fire equipment must be kept up [5].

### 4.5 How herds rotate

* NAU camp systems, camps per herd and rest periods are in the livestock skill. Headline: NAU recommends 6 camps per herd at moderate stocking for most of the country [1].
* South African comparison: ProAgri suggests 4 camps per herd as a minimum, 6 to 8 preferred, herds of 30 to 90 cows, and camps preferably not over 250 ha (exceptions on farms of 10,000 ha or more) [12][13].
* **Holistic / high density example (Springbockvley [4]):**
  * Herds were combined into fewer, bigger mixed "flerds" (sheep plus cattle).
  * Growing season: 4 to 6 days per camp if growth is fast. Dormant season: 7 to 12 days per camp, one round of a cell in about 180 days, two rounds before the rains.
  * Sheep at most 5 to 14 days in a camp and not back within 60 days, which breaks parasite cycles.
  * Calculated density in an average 160 ha camp is about 15 animals/ha (7/ha in the 330 ha camp, 50/ha in the 45 ha camp).
  * Forage is assessed at the start of the non growing season with 2 to 3 samples per camp, planning 300 days including a drought reserve.
  * Result: almost 40 kg liveweight per ha in 2010 and about 11 kg/ha meat a year since 2000 (peak 14.8 kg/ha in 2003).
* **Low density example:** Farm Agagia used a low density four camp approach (from the earlier lesson and skill, [1]).
* Moving: cattle follow the routine of water. At Springbockvley sheep drink early morning and late afternoon and leave; cattle hang around the water from late morning to early afternoon to drink, rest and ruminate [4]. That midday window is the natural time to shut a gate and move them.

## 5. Daily and weekly work, and the bakkie

### 5.1 Sourced pieces

* Check every herd for wellbeing, water and lick at least every second day; a lot of time on border fences [4].
* Staff reach animals and water on foot, by car, tractor or motorbike, or horseback [4].
* Windmills: grease the rod guides and reefing collar about once a week; oil outside pins and pulleys monthly; refill the grease cap once a year. Never climb without a harness; tie the wheel with a rope or chain first [3].
* Solar: wipe dust and bird droppings off panels regularly and adjust for sun angle [3].
* Air valves on pipelines: check regularly for scale and insect nests [3].
* Reservoirs and troughs: repair leaks properly; drain, clean and let dry at least 14 days before chemical repair [3].
* NAU repair rule: "Do not repair provisionally (with a piece of wire). If you repair, do it properly." Keep service records [3].
* Vehicles: older vehicles need an oil and filter change every 5,000 km (modern 10,000 km); check radiator water daily; grass seed clogs radiators [3].
* Cattle need unhindered water all day; NAU warns that early morning and late afternoon kraaling interferes with grazing [2].
* Annual crush work: compulsory vaccination once a year for all cattle, plus branding, tagging, weighing before sale [4]. The NAU monthly programme lists the vaccinations, weaning, branding and weighing by month [2].
* Calving season: carry a pocket "kraal book" to note calves born [2].
* Labour law: job descriptions are not legally required but advised, one page, and must cover all duties the worker may be asked to do [28]. Minimum wage for farm workers N$14/h from 1 Jan 2026 [31] (from the skill).
* Phone signal is often absent in camps (skill, [36]).

### 5.2 A plausible weekly rhythm (Common practice, not sourced; for game design)

| When | Job | Vehicle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Daily or every second day | Water round: windmill turning or solar running, reservoir level, ball valve working, trough clean and full, no dead animals in the water; look at the herd at the water and count it | Bakkie or motorbike along the fence roads |
| Every second day | Lick check and top up at each herd's trough | Bakkie loaded with 50 kg bags |
| Weekly | Grease windmills; fence patrol of the border and the camps in use; fix holes made by warthogs, kudu or porcupines | Bakkie with wire, droppers, pliers, spade |
| On planned dates | Move herd to next camp through corner gates or down the corridor | On foot, horse or motorbike; bakkie to open gates ahead |
| Monthly | Oil windmill pins; check air valves; read rain gauge totals; restock lick and diesel in town | Bakkie (and trailer) to town |
| Seasonal | Lick switch (Jan, Apr in the NAU example), vaccination days at the crush, weaning, weighing, loading for auction or abattoir | Truck from the buyer or a hired transporter at the loading ramp |

## 6. Design notes for the sim

Tag: **S** = backed by a source above. **A** = my assumption for gameplay.

Map and layout
1. (S) A typical map has a handful to a few dozen camps. 4 camps on 10,000 ha [5] up to 60 camps on 9,500 ha [4]. Offer "few big camps" and "many small camps" as player strategies.
2. (S) Camps of about 45 to 330 ha, average 160 ha, in a holistic layout [4]; ProAgri suggests keeping camps under 250 ha [13].
3. (S) Water points are shared: 4 to 5 camps around one reservoir and trough [4], or a central water kraal [2]. Build the map as "wagon wheels" around water.
4. (S) Long narrow camps with water at one end should show overgrazing near the water and unused far corners [1].
5. (S) Show a piosphere ring around each trough: bare inside about 30 m, then a thinning zone; degradation spreads with stocking pressure and is very slow to recover [6][20].
6. (A) Scale distances down for fun: one sim tile ring per few hundred metres. Keep max walk to water inside a camp under about 2 km in real terms [12].
7. (S) Homestead, main kraal and loading ramp near each other, with the ramp reachable from the public road [4]; (A) a second outlying crush becomes a mid game upgrade.

Water
8. (S) Pump choices: windmill (no fuel, weekly grease, needs big reservoir for calm days), solar (no fuel, daytime only, theft risk, clean panels), diesel (on demand, fuel cost) [3][8][9].
9. (S) Cattle drink about 50 L a day; reservoirs should hold about 2 weeks [2][3]. (A) Show reservoir as a bar that falls by herd size x 50 L per day minus pump input.
10. (S) Low yield boreholes favour solar; pump at no more than 60% of tested yield [3][8]. (A) Over pumping can make a borehole "tired" for a season.
11. (S) Leaking reservoirs, broken ball valves and dead birds in water are real events [3]. (A) Use them as random daily faults the player spots on the water round.
12. (S) Borehole registration and abstraction licence exist [21]. (A) Make a "register borehole" admin task, not a blocker.

Lick
13. (S) Two lick seasons: phosphate (P6) on green veld, protein/urea in the dry season; switch decision in January and April in the NAU calendar [2].
14. (S) Use intake per head per day: 100 to 200 g phosphate, about 375 to 600 g winter lick, 1,000 to 1,500 g energy lick [10].
15. (S) Urea lick left in rain can kill cattle [2][30]. (A) A warning event "rain forecast, cover urea troughs".
16. (S) Lick placed away from water spreads grazing [14][19]. (A) Let the player drag lick troughs; better veld use further from water, more bakkie km.

Handling and sales
17. (S) Crush 6 to 18 m, facing south, 70 to 75 cm wide; ramp no steeper than 1:4; gates in corners [2].
18. (S) Sale needs NamLITS registration, a movement permit per farm of origin, and the 90/40 report for Meatco [23][24]. (A) A short checklist mini game before the truck arrives.
19. (S) Low stress handling (from the side, release pressure) is a real technique [4]. (A) Reward calm herding with lower bruising/condemnation.

Movement and work
20. (S) Corridors about 15 m wide move cattle with little labour and double as firebreaks [2].
21. (S) Cattle linger at water late morning to early afternoon [4]. (A) That is the best time to close a water kraal gate and catch them.
22. (S) Check water and lick at least every second day [4]. (A) The core daily loop is the water round in the bakkie.
23. (S) Weekly windmill grease, monthly oiling, yearly grease cap [3].
24. (A) Fence damage from game and porcupines appears at random; a patched fence "with a piece of wire" breaks again sooner (NAU warns against it [3]).
25. (A) Bakkie fuel and km as a resource; lick bags weigh 50 kg [10], so a load limit per trip makes route planning interesting.

## 7. Gaps and unsure points

1. **No official Namibian walking distance or water point spacing standard for commercial farms.** Namibian figures are from communal Kalahari studies (7 km grazing, 7 km ministry spacing for communal points) [6][7]. Commercial distances come from SA (1 to 2 km) and Australia (2 to 5 km).
2. **Lick trough placement in Namibia** (at water vs away from water) is not stated in any Namibian source I found.
3. **Lick fill frequency:** only "every day available" (NAU) and "check at least every second day" (one farm). No standard interval.
4. **Intake figures** are one supplier's label guidance [10]; actual intake varies a lot by area [2].
5. **Windmill vs solar share:** no national survey of what commercial farms use today.
6. **Theft figure** (N$120 million) is from a search summary only [29].
7. **Hot weather water intake** for cattle in Namibia: no sourced peak figure beyond "about 50 L".
8. **Handling facility siting** relative to homestead and roads: only one farm example [4] plus US guidance [27]; no Namibian standard.
9. **Truck capacity, transport cost per km, auction commission:** not found. Agra pages blocked my fetch [26].
10. **Water Act detail:** whether commercial stock watering is "reasonable own use" is unclear; I did not read the full Act [22].
11. **Costs are dated:** borehole with windmill and reservoir about N$100,000, fence about N$6,000/km, and about N$1.38 million fixed improvements for a 5,000 ha farm are 2008/2011 manual figures [3].
12. **Worker routine table (5.2)** is assembled common practice, not a sourced schedule, except where cited.
13. **US lane manure figure (15%)** came from a search summary of a Missouri guide; not used as a key fact.
14. **Outbreak rules change.** Recheck the newest DVS notice before showing any sale rule as current [32].
